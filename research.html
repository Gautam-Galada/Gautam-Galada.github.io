Research

Language models routinely work out things their users never said: an age, a gender, a health condition, a place. My work asks two connected questions about those inferences.

The first is mechanistic. Where inside a model are such inferences represented, and can attribution methods trace the circuits that carry them faithfully? The second is legal. When privacy law forbids a model from using or revealing what it has inferred, does that constraint reach the computation, or only the words the model chooses to print?

I work with the Lawful Computing Lab at the University of North Texas, which builds methods and systems that help computing comply with technology law.

    Synthesis Smuggling: Maliciously Hiding Inferred Personal Attributes in LLM Outputs

    Gautam Galada, Supreeth Shastri, Sagnik Ray Choudhury

    arXiv:2611.17280 [cs.CL], November 2026

    We describe a threat model in which a language model infers personal attributes from routine, assistance-seeking conversations and hides them in content the user later shares, such as an email or a social-media post. We train two open-weight models to infer gender and age group from conversational cues and encode them with invisible characters or word choice, showing that shared model outputs can carry personal inferences even when the model has no direct line to an adversary.
